# The chef-facing pricing sheet

This is the document the chef reads. She is being told what her food will sell for at a price she
did not set, by people taking 45% of it. The sheet has to survive that reading.

## Structure

1. **Header** — her name, item count, kitchen hours, and the split in one line
2. **A short framing box** — her rate is worked out first and the customer price is built up from
   it, so what reaches her is exactly what was agreed
3. **The split bar** — a single horizontal bar showing chef 55% / Zomato 27% / Bhookle 18%,
   colour-coded, with the note that both commissions come out of the customer price rather than
   out of each other. People misread this constantly
4. **The item table** — dish name, then quantity and description as subtext beneath it, then
   customer pays / you get / Zomato gets / Bhookle gets. Colour the chef column consistently so it
   reads as hers all the way down
5. **The rationale** — everything below

No total row. Nobody orders the whole menu, and a total invites the wrong comparison.

## The rationale section

This is what turns a price list into an argument. Four parts:

**We divide, we do not add.** Work it through in her own numbers. "Your rate is ₹120. Adding 45%
gives ₹174, and after commissions only ₹96 reaches you — ₹24 short. Dividing by 55% gives ₹219, and
₹120.45 reaches you." Concrete beats abstract here.

**What kitchens near you charge.** A small table: two or three named local places, their rating,
and two or three real prices each. Named and specific — "Khanna's, 4.2 stars, ₹289 for two kulchas
with gravy" carries weight that "market rates" does not.

**Where your menu sits.** State the gap explicitly. "Twenty of your 31 dishes are ₹219 or less.
Your paratha combos are ₹182–219 against Khanna's ₹289 for a comparable plate, from a kitchen that
has not yet earned its first rating. That gap is what gets a new listing its first orders."

**Anything that changed, and what it cost her.** If rates dropped, show before and after in both
her rate and the customer price, with the reason per row. Write it straight: *"This does reduce
your rate on these three, and we would rather say so plainly than leave you with three dishes
nobody buys."* Anything softer reads as evasion when she works it out later.

## Two optional additions that earn their space

**The per-serving gain ticker.** Where you can compute what she nets today, put
`▲ ₹8.95/serving` under the chef price in green. It answers "am I better off" without her doing
arithmetic. Footnote the assumed commission rate, because the smallest gains flip if it is wrong.

**One open question.** If something needs her judgement — a portion that looks generous against a
local equivalent — ask it rather than deciding it. "RRoyal sells six poori with chole for ₹129.
Yours is three at twice that. We think the answer is the portion rather than the rate. Would four
or five work? Your call — you know what the plate should look like." It signals the sheet is a
conversation, and she usually has the better answer.

## Tone

Plain, warm, specific. No jargon — "gross-up", "basis", "margin" are internal words. She is a
professional running a kitchen; write to that, without either talking down or hiding behind
finance vocabulary.
